ENQUADRE
Therapy CMR

ENQUADRE is a mobile app that helps therapists manage their clients with ease and empathy. It combines scheduling, note-taking, and client management into one warm, human-centered interface.

My goal was to design an experience that feels professional yet personal — helping therapists focus on what truly matters: their clients.

Personas with Interview insights

Therapists juggle multiple clients, sessions, and notes — often across scattered tools. I conducted interviews with independent therapists to uncover pain points in their workflow.

How might we help therapists organize their practice seamlessly while keeping a sense of warmth and humanity in the process?

Some wireframes

I explored different ways to merge scheduling and note-taking into a cohesive workflow. Sketches and wireframes helped refine navigation and visual hierarchy.

The visual direction was guided by warmth, energy, and trust. I used orange and yellow as accent colors, paired with bold black outlines to create a friendly but confident tone.

The final UI focuses on clarity and emotional warmth — minimizing friction for therapists and creating a sense of flow throughout their day.

Some mockups

I prototyped core interactions to make ENQUADRE feel intuitive and alive.

I tested the prototype with 5 therapists and gathered feedback on clarity and navigation. Key improvements focused on simplifying the note-taking flow and improving visibility of upcoming sessions.

ENQUADRE helped demonstrate how warmth and professionalism can coexist in a productivity app.

LOVE
Observatory Operations GUI

LOVE (LSST Operators Visualization Environment) is a crucial GUI developed to meet the specific requirements of Observers and Experts at the Vera C. Rubin Observatory. As a developer and UX expert, I had the opportunity to closely collaborate with system experts and observers to create tailored components for their needs. The software allows users to remotely operate both the Main and Auxiliary telescopes, while also providing a visualization platform for the incredibly large amount of data fed into the system.

LOVE, as part of the Vera C. Rubin Observatory, serves as one of the most fundamental pieces for its successful operation. This giant telescope boasts over 65 systems and subsystems, demanding constant monitoring by Science Experts and Operators. The unique challenge was designing for components that were still under construction. I take immense pride and happiness in being part of such a remarkable scientific and technological endeavor.

Managing the cognitive load of observers was a significant focus, considering the Observatory comprises more than 65 systems and subsystems, with over 50k telemetries being published every second.

Users had to be able to Monitor, Operate, and Study through LOVE

The Design Process

One of the main challenges was developing LOVE simultaneously with the construction of the Observatory, leading to a lack of clarity regarding requirements and a constantly evolving context. This was exacerbated by the fact that the Observatory involved continuous improvements in designing, building, and implementing different instruments. To mitigate this, the design process for the development of the new LOVE component involved a systematic approach comprising four key phases.

Phase 1: Research & Documentation
In the initial phase, the team conducted extensive research and documented information related to the device that the new component would monitor. This involved gathering data on the device's usage and reviewing design papers. Once enough information was collected to grasp the basics, interviews were conducted with experts responsible for designing and building these devices. This aimed to gain a profound understanding of the device's importance, the type of data and information deemed crucial by experts, and insights from operators and observers regarding what they considered essential to visualize. Assimilating these insights formed a foundational understanding of the device's functionality and user expectations.

Phase 2: Information Mapping and Collaborative Sketching
Building upon the insights gained in the research phase, the focus shifted to creating visual representations that mapped the flow of information and user journeys. This phase also aimed to illustrate the intricate relationships between the various elements of the complex devices. Once the information architecture was untangled, and the main objectives of the component were identified, the team engaged in collaborative sketching sessions to produce wireframes. This collaborative process fostered creativity and collective input, refining ideas and ensuring a holistic understanding of the component.

Diagram of the 4 phase design process

Phase 3: Mockups and Design Validation
Detailed mockups based on the wireframes and diagrams from the previous phase were created. This step involved fine-tuning design elements through close collaboration with users, experts, and developers. Design validation was a key aspect, ensuring alignment with end-user expectations and technical feasibility. Iterative feedback loops during this phase were crucial for refining the design and addressing potential challenges.

Phase 4: Implementation Preparation
With the validated design in hand, the final phase focused on preparing all elements for seamless implementation by the development team. This included creating a comprehensive set of design specifications, documentation, and assets. Clear communication and collaboration with the development team were paramount to ensure a smooth transition from design to implementation.

By diligently progressing through these four phases, our team successfully navigated the design process, combining research, collaboration, and validation to deliver a well-crafted LOVE component ready for implementation.

The different steps for the M2 component

The Implementation Process

We adopted an agile development methodology, leveraging the implementation of an MVP that was strategically released into a dedicated test area. The continuous integration process, orchestrated through GitHub and Jenkins, allowed users to validate the component iteratively. This approach ensured a dynamic and responsive development cycle, fostering adaptability and swift adjustments based on user feedback.

However, even with a successful final release, the dynamic nature of the project, the observatory being still in the construction phase, occasionally led to changes in scope during the verification process. While this is a common aspect of evolving projects, it did necessitate additional work. These challenges prompted us to address these pitfalls of evolving requirements and refine the design and implementation system for optimal performance.

Visits to the Observatory were a MUST

Testing

Rigorous testing procedures were implemented to validate the functionality and performance of the GUI. One noteworthy aspect of our testing strategy was the recreation of the proposed control room and the creation of a small robotic telescope—a model Main Telescope—to simulate real-world operations and thoroughly assess various features while the real thing was being built.

To replicate the conditions of the actual Main Telescope, we meticulously designed and constructed a scaled-down robotic version. This model emulated basic aspects of the Main Telescope's functionalities, allowing us to simulate commands, data transfers, and responses within a controlled environment.

Testing was an iterative process, with each phase informing subsequent refinements to the GUI and the testing environment. Continuous feedback loops were established to address any issues identified during testing promptly. This approach allowed us to fine-tune LOVE to meet the dynamic demands of observatory operations effectively.

Our model Simonyi Telescope and LOVE on our Control Room Replica at CCLM

While the dynamic nature of the Observatory's construction occasionally led to changes in scope during verification, these challenges were met with a proactive approach. Adapting to evolving requirements, refining designs, and optimizing the implementation system became essential for maintaining optimal performance.

I am profoundly grateful to have had the opportunity to contribute to something as groundbreaking as LOVE for the Vera C. Rubin Observatory. Working on this project has been a source of joy and fulfillment, and I am privileged to have collaborated with a team of dedicated individuals in the pursuit of advancing scientific exploration. As the Observatory continues to evolve, so too does LOVE, a testament to the collaborative spirit and adaptability of our team in the face of dynamic challenges.

Smart Agriculture
Designing Human-Centered Interfaces for AI in Farming

In recent years, agriculture has seen a growing interest in AI-powered decision-making. I worked on two applied projects that aimed to translate complex machine learning and computer vision models into practical, usable tools for farmers and winemakers. My role as a UX Expert was to ensure that advanced data pipelines translated into clear insights, actionable alerts, and intuitive workflows.

Some results and documents during Discovery

Discovery & Research

As with any good design story, it all starts with research. Past projects had shown me how easy it is to chase the loudest problem instead of the right one. To avoid this, I created a Field Guide document to structure our Discovery process. Through interviews, workshops, and contextual research, I developed personas representing agricultural stakeholders—from vineyard managers to field technicians. These personas captured their goals, pain points, and levels of technological confidence, grounding our design choices in real-world needs.

People need to understand, and like, diagrams

Making the Invisible Visible

The heart of both projects was machine learning and computer vision—systems that, by nature, are invisible to end users. To bridge this gap, I designed pipeline diagrams and process flows that explained how predictions (for frost, yield, grape growth stages, and harvest timing) were generated. These visual tools became essential not only for farmers and winemakers, but also for alignment between data scientists, agronomists, and business stakeholders. By making the ML process understandable, I helped ensure trust in the system's outputs.

Some sessions had to be Virtual, since users where on-site farming.

Collaboration & Iteration

These were complex, multi-stakeholder projects. To keep everyone aligned, I facilitated feedback and review sessions using tools like Miro. Agile rituals such as user story mapping and sprint reviews helped us break down large technical challenges into achievable increments. These sessions were also key for managing expectations—making sure stakeholders understood what could realistically be delivered, and when.

Part of the Frost Forecasting dashboard

Designing for Critical Moments

One of the most impactful outcomes was the FrostForecast dashboard. The design focused on alerts, since frost events require immediate action and farmers are not always online. Field observations revealed that users needed at least four hours of advance warning to mobilize mitigation tools like wind machines or sprinklers. This insight hadn't surfaced during early interviews, but emerged later through direct observation—a reminder of the importance of iterative research throughout the design process. The final dashboard provided not just data, but timely, actionable insights that directly protected crops.

Testing and debugging on-site with real situations was a must for me.

On-Site Validation

Finally, I tested prototypes on-site with real users in vineyards and fields. This grounded the design in the actual working environments where these tools would be used—outdoors, often under pressure, with limited connectivity. Observing how users interacted with the dashboard in context gave me critical feedback to refine information hierarchy, alert delivery, and overall usability.
